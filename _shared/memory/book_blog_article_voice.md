# book_blog_article_voice.md

This file records stable preferences for how explanatory non-academic prose
should sound across book chapters, blog posts, and long-form articles.
Read it before drafting or revising `BOOK` material, or when the user wants
popular-science prose in the David-and-Tom style.

Keep this file focused on stable voice preferences. Put project-specific
argument choices, chapter structure, and open decisions in each project's
`memory.md` or planning files.

## How to maintain this file

- Add only preferences that are stable across book, blog, and article work.
- Prefer concrete rules over generic descriptors like "engaging" or "readable".
- Anchor rules with short positive examples from the user's own texts where possible.
- Mark inferences as `[INFERENCE]` if they are derived from repeated patterns rather than explicit instructions.
- Keep project-specific chapter beats out of this file unless they have become general method.

## Voice snapshot

- Desired impression: intelligent, conversational, lightly comic, and analytically honest.
- Default tone: warm, explanatory, and wry rather than performatively funny.
- Default audience: smart general readers who do not need prior economics or statistics training.
- One-sentence description of the voice: start from an ordinary or vivid thing, show how a hidden mechanism distorts it, and explain the stakes in plain English without flattening the complexity.
- Punctuation is part of the voice, not cleanup after the fact; the books use punctuation to carry timing, irony, and shifts in register.
- Calibration source: when revising toward house style, use the user's own books and strongest current chapters as the first reference point. Generic de-AI cleanup comes after that.

## Sentence-level preferences

- Preferred sentence length: mix medium explanatory sentences with short pivots and occasional long comic or cumulative sentences. Across the source books, sentence length is often around the mid-20-word range, but the important thing is variation rather than a fixed target.
- Preferred balance of short vs. long sentences: mostly short-to-medium for pace, with longer sentences used for escalation, lists, or comic payoff. A blunt short sentence often follows a longer one.
- First-person usage: prefer `we` when guiding the reader, making a concession, or admitting uncertainty; use `you` sparingly but comfortably for direct reader address.
- Hedging level: low for clear explanation, moderate for contested claims, explicit when evidence is mixed.
- Preferred transition style: use clean spoken transitions such as `but`, `so`, `because`, `after all`, `in practice`, and `the trouble is`.
- Preferred verb style: active and concrete; prefer verbs that show people gaming, bending, dodging, noticing, or exploiting systems.
- Preferred treatment of jargon: name the concept early, but only after showing the reader what it feels like in practice.
- Number formatting: use numerals when the magnitude matters; otherwise keep the prose natural.
- Avoid over-tidying: do not automatically split every clause into separate neat declarative sentences if the source voice would rather carry the thought through with punctuation.

## Punctuation and cadence preferences

- Use semicolons freely when two clauses belong to the same thought and the second clause sharpens, reverses, or deadpans the first.
- Use colons for turns into examples, definitions, lists, or payoffs. In the source books, colons are common and often do real argumentative work.
- Use parentheses for dry caveats, clarifications, jokes, or human side-notes that should stay in the sentence without taking over it.
- Use rhetorical questions regularly, then answer them quickly. The books often stage the reader's objection in question form before resolving it.
- Use the occasional one-sentence paragraph or very short follow-up sentence for emphasis.
- Use comma-heavy list rhythm when stacking examples, contrasts, or comic escalation.
- Exclamation marks are allowed, but only in clearly spoken, comic, or quoted moments; they are not the default energy source.
- Think in beats, not just grammar: setup; complication; aside; payoff. The punctuation often marks those beats more than the wording does.
- If a passage reads too smooth, too symmetrical, or too copy-edited, it is probably under-punctuated for this voice.

## Paragraph-level preferences

- Opening move: begin with a scene, puzzle, claim, rule, anecdote, or familiar frustration before turning abstract.
- Typical paragraph structure: concrete setup -> hidden mechanism -> widening implication -> brief payoff.
- Preferred signposting: explain why the detail matters as you go rather than after a long abstract detour.
- Preferred paragraph length: short to medium; break before the reader gets lost.
- Ending move: end sections with a crystallising observation, not a recap.

## Explanation preferences

- Show the pattern before giving the label.
- Use worked examples with moving parts the reader can picture.
- Prefer domestic, sporting, bureaucratic, or cultural examples over invented abstractions when possible.
- Keep technical detail in the main text only if it improves intuition; otherwise compress or box it off.
- Use "why this matters" paragraphs to reconnect theory to ordinary life, institutions, or incentives.
- For a mixed UK/US audience, lightly translate highly British examples or terms in the sentence itself; do not stop the prose dead for cultural explanation, but do not assume every reader knows the reference.
- Avoid turning the book's title into an in-chapter catchphrase, label, diagnosis, or payoff. For *The Target Trap*, do not use `the target trap` in chapter body text unless the user explicitly asks for title/front-matter language.

## Humour and stance

- Use light self-deprecation, amused honesty, and wry understatement.
- Treat the reader as smart company, not as a pupil being lectured.
- Allow brief asides, but make sure they accelerate the prose rather than interrupt it.
- Do not joke in ways that undercut genuinely high-stakes material.
- Avoid sneering or showing off.
- Do not lean too heavily on recurring cute analogy templates; one memorable phrase is fine, but repeated variants start to feel like the authors are entertaining themselves.

## Structure by format

- Book chapter: give the opening scene room to breathe, work at least one example through fully, then widen to several adjacent cases.
- Draft-stage book chapters should be lean argument structures, not fully embellished final prose. Around 2,000 words can be a good target when the purpose is to expose the bones of the argument for Tom to develop later. Prioritise clear structure, examples, transitions, and payoffs over connective filler or excessive handholding.
- Book chapter headings should be sparse. Avoid long runs of short subsection beats that make a chapter read like an outline or memo rather than a flowing chapter.
- Long-form article: reach the nut graf earlier, usually within the first few paragraphs, while keeping a strong hook.
- Blog post: tighten the opening and keep section turns visible; it can sound slightly more direct and provisional than a book chapter.
- Across all three: keep the mechanism, the stakes, and the human behavior visible on the page.

## Evidence and inference preferences

- Do not invent facts, quotes, or citations.
- When moving from a concrete example to a general pattern, make the scope legible.
- Prefer explicit phrases such as `this suggests`, `the pattern is`, or `the trouble is` when widening from case to claim.
- Keep the distinction between what happened, why it happened, and what it implies.
- Preserve the question-answer movement of the source texts; they often ask what sounds like a casual question and use the answer to advance the argument.

## Anti-AI cleanup preferences

- Treat de-AI editing as meaning-preserving polish, not detector gaming.
- Remove stock chatbot signposting and managerial framing: `here's the thing`, `let's dive in`, `what this means is`, `in today's world`, and `it is important to note`.
- Avoid over-built symmetry: rule-of-three phrasing, repeated `not just X, but Y` constructions, and question-then-immediate-answer on every turn.
- Repeat the clearest word when it carries the scene or mechanism. Do not reach for forced synonyms simply to avoid repetition.
- Keep some irregularity. If every paragraph lands with the same cadence, the prose sounds processed rather than written.

## Style to avoid

- Opening with a dry definition before the reader has anything to care about.
- Abstract throat-clearing about "complex modern societies" before showing a concrete instance.
- Replacing scenes with management jargon or policy-paper diction.
- Over-explaining the moral rather than letting the example do the work.
- Sounding arch, smug, or too pleased with the cleverness of the analogy.
- Reusing a successful rhetorical mould too often, especially costume-joke lines such as `X is Goodhart's Law in a wig` or nearby variants.
- Flattening the prose into uniformly competent, evenly punctuated explanatory sentences.
- Treating semicolons, colons, parentheses, and rhetorical questions as optional ornament rather than part of the sentence music.
- Obvious AI defaults: padded transitions, polished but empty throat-clearing, and summary sentences that announce the meaning before the example earns it.

## Positive examples

Add short excerpts from the user's own texts that capture the target voice.
For each excerpt, add one line saying why it works.

1. "Imagine a kitchen in a shared space: an office, or a student house. Inevitably, the kitchen sink keeps filling up with dirty plates and mugs."
Reason: opens with an ordinary, visual, faintly comic scene that everyone recognises before any theory appears.

2. "The human race has eradicated smallpox; it has travelled to the moon; it has peered into the heart of the atom. But we still cannot get people to wash their own bloody dishes."
Reason: escalates from grand human achievement to petty domestic failure for comic force and thematic clarity.

3. "As soon as your partner asks you to pick up some groceries on the way back from work, you have created a principal-agent problem."
Reason: translates an abstract economics concept into an immediate household situation in one sentence.

4. "How do you write a bestselling book? There's a formula, apparently, or an algorithm, perhaps; or it may be a secret code."
Reason: starts with a hooky question, then uses rhythm and mild mockery to set a skeptical, lively tone.

5. "Spoiler alert: it's a mistake. It's a very similar mistake to the Texas sharpshooter error discussed in the previous chapter, but with subtle and important distinctions. To understand it, let's talk about Second World War bombers, because that's fun."
Reason: moves briskly from verdict to explanation while using a playful aside to make an intellectual transition feel easy.

6. "Because that would obviously be absurd. Football is not a laboratory experiment. You do not actually care whether the striker's armpit is closer to goal than the defender's backside."
Reason: sharpens the underlying principle by saying plainly what the reader already feels, with precision and comic bluntness.

7. "That's the nuanced position, but you're not going to write a book saying that."
Reason: acknowledges complexity without pretending that good explanatory prose must sound bloodless or over-qualified.

8. "There are few things so dispiriting as reading on a Kindle and realising that after 30 minutes you've only gone from 3 per cent to 4 per cent."
Reason: turns a point about scale into an everyday sensory complaint rather than a sterile statistic.

9. "It is the problem that the letter of the law and the spirit of the law are not the same thing, as Saint Paul realised, two thousand years ago."
Reason: closes the loop between everyday example and deeper intellectual frame in a compact, memorable sentence.

10. "We are not alone, stranded on a desert island; we have to work, live, and interact with other people every day."
Reason: uses the semicolon to keep the thought moving in one breath rather than flattening it into two separate statements.

11. "A classic example: good schools tend to churn out children who get high grades."
Reason: the colon turns a general claim into a concrete example immediately, without a baggy transition.

12. "What number should we use?"
Reason: very short question sentences are used to pull the reader into the next step of the argument.

## Negative examples and rewrites

- Avoid: "This chapter examines the tension between formal rules and informal norms in competitive environments."
- Prefer: start with the rule, the argument, or the incident itself.
- Reason: the abstract summary drains the life out of the material before the reader has a reason to care.

- Avoid: "In modern societies, incentives often generate unintended consequences across a range of institutional settings."
- Prefer: name the setting and show the consequence.
- Reason: generic institutional language sounds like a policy memo, not a live piece of explanatory prose.

- Avoid: "It is important to note that the issue is complicated."
- Prefer: show the complication directly, or say "the trouble is..." and explain it.
- Reason: the warning is weaker than the explanation.

- Avoid: "This is understandable. The offside rule has a reputation for being very complicated. In fact it is only slightly complicated."
- Prefer: "This is understandable, because the offside law has a reputation for being impossibly complicated when in fact it is merely slightly complicated."
- Reason: the source voice often keeps the comic reversal inside one sentence rather than stepping through it in tidy fragments.

- Avoid: "Here's the thing: modern life is full of hidden incentives."
- Prefer: "Modern life is full of hidden incentives, though we usually notice them only when something goes wrong."
- Reason: cut stock signposting and let the claim arrive as prose rather than as a stage direction.
