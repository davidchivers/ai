# academic_voice.md

This file records stable preferences for how academic prose should sound.
Read it before drafting or revising paper text, introductions, abstracts,
conclusions, literature reviews, and referee responses.

Keep this file focused on stable voice preferences. Put project-specific
substance, framing, and open decisions in each project's `memory.md`.

## How to maintain this file

- Add only preferences that are stable across papers.
- Prefer concrete rules over vague descriptors.
- When possible, anchor rules with short positive and negative examples.
- If a preference is tentative, mark it as `[PROVISIONAL]`.
- If a preference applies only to one project, keep it out of this file.

## Voice snapshot

- Desired impression: clear, analytic, accessible without sounding popularized, and relevant to the paper's audience. State policy relevance only when the paper supports it.
- Default tone: formal, explanatory, and measured.
- Default audience: the target journal's academic readers, with policy readers included when the paper speaks directly to them.
- One-sentence description of the voice: define the phenomenon or environment plainly, make the evidential status of each claim clear, explain mechanisms only as far as the design supports them, and connect results to concrete magnitudes or scope.
- Calibration source: when possible, calibrate sentence rhythm, citation handling, and level of explicitness to the user's own published papers and strongest prior drafts. Generic anti-AI cleanup is secondary to house style.

## Sentence-level preferences

- Preferred sentence length: target one causal or inferential step per sentence, typically 20-35 words. Split when the mechanism, evidence, or epistemic status changes.
- Preferred balance of short vs. long sentences: mostly long explanatory sentences, with occasional short framing statements.
- First-person usage: use "I" or "we" for contribution and methodology statements according to the paper's authorship and established voice; use active impersonal constructions for mechanisms; use passive voice mainly when it clarifies data construction.
- Hedging level: state model mechanics directly; use proportionate language for estimates, descriptive associations, interpretation, and out-of-model extrapolation. Never let confident prose imply identification the design does not provide.
- Preferred transition style: explicit logical connectors such as "as", "thus", "for example", "instead of", and "based on", but only when they do real argumentative work. Avoid empty signposting such as "this section explores" or "here is what matters".
- Preferred verb style: use active verbs for incentives, constraints, choice, measurement, and comparison; use simple present for general mechanisms and definitions.
- Preferred treatment of jargon: define the label immediately on first use, then explain the mechanism, measure, or comparison in plain language.
- Use "margin" sparingly. Keep it for formal choice boundaries or comparative-static variation; otherwise prefer the concrete object ("composition," "channel," "firm type," "entry," "scale," "tax financing," or "search effort").
- Number formatting: spell out small numbers in prose ("three periods"); use numerals for magnitudes and calibration ("15 percent", "41 periods").
- Punctuation: prefer commas, parentheses, colons, semicolons, and full stops. Do not use em dashes in academic prose.

## Paragraph-level preferences

- Topic sentence style: open with a direct statement of the phenomenon, model, empirical comparison, result, or mechanism under discussion.

## Negative examples and rewrites

- Avoid: "This paper very clearly proves..."
- Prefer: "These results suggest..."
- Reason: keeps the claim proportionate to the evidence.

- Avoid: "It is important to note that..."
- Prefer: just state the thing directly.
- Reason: filler phrase that delays the content.

- Avoid: "This is a novel contribution because..."
- Prefer: state the theoretical departure, new measure, data contribution, or empirical comparison and let novelty be implicit.
- Reason: novelty claims sound defensive; the precise substantive difference does the same work.

- Avoid: "This change in their status from being a renter to a homeowner (who is unlikely to make additional purchases of housing) will lead them to switch their political support from lower to higher prices for the rest of their life."
- Prefer: "Once a renter becomes a homeowner, their incentive flips: additional supply now depresses the value of their asset. From that point forward, they vote to restrict new construction."
- Reason: split at the state change so the causal step has room to breathe.

- Avoid: "Housing supply remains a pivotal issue in today's policy landscape."
- Prefer: "Housing supply is constrained in many US metro areas."
- Reason: cut inflation and name the object directly.

- Avoid: "This mechanism not only affects prices but also shapes political support."
- Prefer: "This mechanism affects prices and shapes political support."
- Reason: remove the template contrast and state the two effects directly.

- Avoid: "The reform raises rents in the short run -- and lowers ownership later."
- Prefer: "The reform raises rents in the short run. It later reduces homeownership."
- Reason: do not use dash stitching where two direct sentences are clearer.

## Session update rule

Only update this file when:

- you explicitly state a writing preference, or
- a pattern appears repeatedly in your edits across more than one paper.

If a new preference conflicts with an old one, replace the old rule instead of
letting both remain active.
