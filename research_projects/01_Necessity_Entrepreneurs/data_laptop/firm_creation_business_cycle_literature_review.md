# Firm creation over the business cycle

Status: sidecar literature note for motivation-figure design and later MIT-shock framing.

Last updated: 2026-03-08

## Scope

Question: what does the literature say about firm creation over the business cycle, and what does that imply for how `01_Necessity_Entrepreneurs` should motivate the final MIT-shock exercise?

## Bottom line

The clean reading of the literature is not simply that "recessions create more firms." A more precise summary is:

- broad entrepreneurship or self-employment can rise in recessions because unemployment pushes some workers into necessity-style entry,
- employer startup activity and startup job creation are more cyclical and can weaken sharply in downturns,
- recession startup cohorts tend to be smaller and have lower growth potential,
- nonemployer or own-account activity should therefore be kept separate from employer-firm creation and entrant size.

This is the main reason the motivation figure should separate `0 employees` from positive-employment bins.

## Verified papers and what they contribute

### 1. Fairlie (2011/2013), entrepreneurship and the Great Recession

Verified source:
- Robert W. Fairlie, "Entrepreneurship, Economic Conditions, and the Great Recession," IZA Discussion Paper 5725 (2011), later published in *Journal of Economics and Management Strategy* (2013).
- Primary source: https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/5725/entrepreneurship-economic-conditions-and-the-great-recession

Core takeaway:
- Higher local unemployment rates increase business creation probabilities in CPS microdata.
- The response is stronger for people who are not initially employed.

Use for this project:
- This is direct support for the idea that recession conditions can raise entry on the necessity margin.
- It does **not** by itself imply stronger employer startup creation or larger entrant firms.

### 2. Fairlie and Fossen (2019), opportunity versus necessity entrepreneurship

Verified source:
- local PDF: `literature/fairlie_fossen_2019.pdf`
- primary NBER page: https://www.nber.org/papers/w26377

Core takeaway:
- Opportunity entrepreneurship is procyclical.
- Necessity entrepreneurship is strongly countercyclical.
- Opportunity and necessity types are associated with different growth orientation.

Use for this project:
- This is probably the closest conceptual bridge from the empirical recession patterns to the paper's mechanism.
- It supports a motivation section that distinguishes "more entry in recessions" from "more growth-oriented firm creation in recessions."

### 3. Fossen (2019/2021), decomposition over the business cycle

Verified source:
- Frank M. Fossen, "Entrepreneurship over the Business Cycle in the United States: A Decomposition," IZA Discussion Paper 12499 (2019); revised version published in *Small Business Economics* (2021).
- Primary source: https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/12499/entrepreneurship-over-the-business-cycle-in-the-united-states-a-decomposition

Core takeaway:
- The rise in entrepreneurial entry during recessions is mostly explained by higher unemployment.
- The countercyclical entry pattern is strong for unincorporated entrepreneurship and weak for incorporated entrepreneurship.

Use for this project:
- This gives a sharper empirical reason to focus the first figure on the `0 employees` or own-account margin rather than treating all startups as one object.
- It also suggests that if the model is meant to speak to necessity entrepreneurship, the empirical comparison should emphasize the low-scale entry margin.

### 4. Sedlacek and Sterk (2017), startup cohorts over the business cycle

Verified source:
- local PDF: `literature/sedlacek_sterk_2017_repository_version.pdf`
- primary article page: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20141280

Core takeaway:
- Startup employment is procyclical.
- Cohort differences persist for years after entry.
- Variation across cohorts is driven largely by startup size and growth potential rather than just the number of startups.

Use for this project:
- This is central for any claim linking the final MIT shock to recession-like entrant composition.
- It points toward testing not only entry rates but also entrant firm size in the model.

### 5. Haltiwanger (2012) and Haltiwanger, Jarmin, and Miranda (2010/2013)

Verified sources:
- John Haltiwanger, "Job Creation and Firm Dynamics in the United States," *Innovation Policy and the Economy* (2012), NBER chapter page: https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/innovation-policy-and-economy-volume-12/job-creation-and-firm-dynamics-united-states
- John C. Haltiwanger, Ron S. Jarmin, and Javier Miranda, "Who Creates Jobs? Small vs. Large vs. Young," NBER Working Paper 16300 (2010), later *Review of Economics and Statistics* (2013): https://www.nber.org/papers/w16300

Core takeaway:
- Startups and young firms are disproportionately important for job creation.
- The recession sharply reduced startup-related job creation.
- Age matters more than size alone for understanding job creation dynamics.

Use for this project:
- This helps justify why the paper should care not only about entry rates but also about the type of firms being created.
- It also warns against using a single aggregate entrepreneurship series as the only empirical motivation.

### 6. Fort, Haltiwanger, Jarmin, and Miranda (2013), firm age, size, and cyclical sensitivity

Verified source:
- Teresa C. Fort, John Haltiwanger, Ron S. Jarmin, and Javier Miranda, "How Firms Respond to Business Cycles: The Role of Firm Age and Firm Size," NBER Working Paper 19134 (2013), later *IMF Economic Review*.
- Primary source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w19134

Core takeaway:
- Young and small businesses are more cycle-sensitive than older and larger businesses.
- Young or small firms were hit especially hard in the Great Recession.

Use for this project:
- This strengthens the case for plotting separate size bins over time.
- If the model is going to speak to recessions, it should say something about whether shocks compress the scale of young or entrant businesses.

### 7. Fairlie and Miranda (2016), transition from nonemployer to employer

Verified source:
- Robert W. Fairlie and Javier Miranda, "Taking the Leap: The Determinants of Entrepreneurs Hiring their First Employee," NBER Working Paper 22428 (2016).
- Primary source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w22428

Core takeaway:
- Most nonemployer startups do not hire.
- When they do hire, it usually happens early.
- Hiring is more common among more growth-oriented nonemployer startups.

Use for this project:
- This is directly relevant for interpreting the `0 employees` line.
- A recession-induced rise in nonemployer entry should not automatically be read as a rise in employer-firm creation.

### 8. Zarutskie and Yang (2017), young firms in the Great Recession

Verified source:
- Rebecca Zarutskie and Tiantian Yang, "How Did Young Firms Fare during the Great Recession? Evidence from the Kauffman Firm Survey," chapter in *Measuring Entrepreneurial Businesses: Current Knowledge and Challenges*.
- Primary source: https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/measuring-entrepreneurial-businesses-current-knowledge-and-challenges/how-did-young-firms-fare-during-great-recession-evidence-kauffman-firm-survey

Core takeaway:
- Young firms experienced slower growth in revenues, employment, and assets during the Great Recession.
- Financing conditions also tightened.

Use for this project:
- This supports a recession narrative centered on smaller and weaker entrant or young-firm cohorts, not just fewer or more entrants.

### 9. Hurst and Pugsley (2011), what small businesses do

Verified source:
- local PDF: `literature/hurst_pugsley_2011.pdf`

Core takeaway:
- Most small businesses are not high-growth innovation projects.
- Nonpecuniary motives and local-service provision are central for many owners.

Use for this project:
- This is useful background for why necessity-style or own-account recession entry may not map into large employer firms.
- It also helps keep the paper from overstating the growth content of all self-employment.

## Synthesis for this paper

The literature points to a three-part empirical distinction:

1. Entry margin:
- broad entrepreneurship and self-employment can be countercyclical because unemployment pushes people into entry.

2. Employer creation and job-creation margin:
- employer startups, startup employment, and young-firm job creation are more procyclical and weaken in recessions.

3. Entrant scale and quality margin:
- recession cohorts tend to be smaller and less growth-oriented, with persistent effects.

For `Necessity_Entrepreneurs`, that suggests the empirical motivation should be framed as:

- recessions may increase low-scale own-account entry,
- but recessions can simultaneously reduce or weaken employer startup creation,
- so the core question is not only whether entry rises, but what kind of businesses are created and how large they are.

## Recommended empirical framing for the paper

### Figure design

Best first-pass figure:
- annual US business counts by employment size with recession shading,
- bins:
  - `0 employees`,
  - `1-4 employees`,
  - `5-19 employees`,
  - `20+ employees`.

Why:
- it visualizes the distinction between own-account or nonemployer activity and employer businesses,
- it aligns with the literature above,
- it gives the cleanest bridge to a necessity-entrepreneurship interpretation.

### Model-to-data bridge

If the final stage is an MIT shock intended to mimic recession-like conditions, the most natural comparison objects are:

- entry into own-account or very small businesses,
- entry into employer firms,
- average entrant scale or firm size,
- possibly transition from nonemployment to entrepreneurship if the model output supports it.

## Cautions

- The planned `0 employees` empirical series is a nonemployer-establishment measure, not a literal CPS self-employment count.
- Positive-employment bins from BDS are establishment counts, not firm counts.
- This is a deliberate comparability choice for the first figure because zero-employee and positive-employment bins then live on a more compatible unit.
- If later we want a person-level motivation figure, we should build a separate CPS-based self-employment series rather than mixing units in one chart.
