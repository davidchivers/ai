# Literature and synthesis

## Motive-based measures

The GEM evidence is useful because it asks directly about startup motives. The local
project notes report that in weighted U.S. TEA microdata for 2019 to 2021, roughly
41 to 50 percent of early-stage entrepreneurs report starting a business because jobs
are scarce, while roughly 65 to 73 percent also report wealth or high-income motives.
That overlap is important evidence. It shows that necessity-type and opportunity-type
signals can coexist inside the same entrepreneurial spell.

The limit is equally clear. Motives are self-reported, not mutually exclusive, and do
not map one-for-one into the paper's counterfactual definition. GEM is therefore best
used as evidence that the measurement problem is real, not as a clean classifier.

## Prior-status measures

Fairlie and Fossen (2019) are the closest existing bridge to the paper. Their
employment-to-business versus nonemployment-to-business split is transparent and easy
to estimate, and it lines up with the paper's emphasis on outside options. Fairlie
(2013) and Fossen (2021) reinforce the same idea: weaker labor-market conditions and
unemployment matter more for lower-scale entry than for more growth-oriented forms of
business creation.

But prior status is still only a proxy. Entering from employment does not guarantee
that the outside option is strong, because workers can anticipate job loss or respond
to deteriorating local conditions before a separation occurs. Entering from
nonemployment does not guarantee necessity either, because some opportunity-driven
founders can pass through temporary nonemployment before launching.

## Business form and scale measures

The project's sidecar work is strongest when it separates low-scale activity from
employer-oriented activity. Fossen (2021), Fairlie and Miranda (2016), and the recent
business-size and business-applications notes all point in the same direction: the
first-worker margin is distinct from own-account entry, and recession conditions can
push these margins in different directions.

This matters because a simple self-employment or entrepreneurship count compresses the
object too much. The paper's own model says the necessity margin should show up most
strongly in nonemployer or very low-scale entry, not in employer startups as a whole.

## Cohort quality and job-creation evidence

Sedlacek and Sterk (2017), Haltiwanger (2012), and related young-firm evidence add a
second lesson. Business-cycle variation is not only about how many firms appear. It is
also about entrant size, job creation, and growth potential. Recession cohorts are
smaller and weaker on the employer margin, even when some extensive-margin entry
remains resilient.

This fits the paper's formal design well. If necessity entrepreneurship is tied to weak
outside options, then the empirical signature should include lower initial scale and a
weaker payroll margin, not just more business ownership.

## Synthesis

No single proxy family matches the formal object on its own.

Motive measures identify stated reasons, not counterfactual choice. Prior-status
measures identify the transition margin, but status is endogenous. Business-form
measures identify scale and employer orientation, but not the cause of entry. Cohort
evidence identifies job-creation content, but not individual sorting directly.

Inference: the best empirical strategy is to combine two ideas rather than force one
proxy to do everything. First, use quasi-exogenous variation in outside options to
identify the margin of entry caused by weaker wage-work prospects. Second, measure that
response separately for nonemployer entry, employer entry, initial payroll, and early
survival.
