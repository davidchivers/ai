# Opportunity self-employment note

## Why this note exists

One of the biggest risks in this project is collapsing "self-employed" into
"necessity" and "employer" into "opportunity." The model itself does not justify that
shortcut, and neither does the empirical literature.

The user concern is exactly right:

- even firms that later become large often start small,
- startup capital can reflect financing constraints rather than type,
- opportunity self-employment plainly exists.

This note makes that point explicit so the empirical design does not drift back into a
too-simple taxonomy.

## Why opportunity self-employment exists

There are at least five economically coherent reasons an opportunity entrepreneur may
remain self-employed, at least initially.

### 1. The project is genuinely attractive at small scale

Some businesses have high private returns even without employees. Independent
professionals, consultants, creators, tradespeople, and many local-service businesses
can be profitable and chosen voluntarily despite good wage-work options.

### 2. Entry is staged under uncertainty

A growth-oriented founder may start alone to test demand, reduce burn, or learn about
the market before hiring. In that case, zero employees at entry reflects sequencing, not
necessarily low ambition or necessity.

### 3. Hiring has a discrete fixed cost

The current paper already uses this logic structurally through `f_hire`. Even if a
project is attractive, the first employee can be delayed by payroll taxes, compliance,
training costs, supervision time, or legal risk.

### 4. Capital constraints blur the signal

Low startup capital can mean weak opportunity, but it can also mean:

- the business technology is light-capital,
- the founder is financially constrained but talented,
- the founder chooses to bootstrap until uncertainty resolves.

So low capital does not cleanly separate necessity from opportunity either.

### 5. Nonpecuniary returns matter

Hurst and Pugsley-style self-employment can be a chosen form of work organization, not
only a fallback. Flexibility, autonomy, and local-service orientation can make
self-employment attractive even for workers with decent outside options.

## What this means for classification

Three common shortcuts fail.

### Shortcut 1: `self-employed = necessity`

This fails because opportunity self-employment exists.

### Shortcut 2: `starts with employees = opportunity`

This is directionally more plausible, but still incomplete. Some necessity-driven
projects may start with employees in family firms, informal partnerships, or sectors
where minimal scale is required.

### Shortcut 3: `small or low-capital = necessity`

This fails because many growth-oriented or opportunity entrepreneurs stage both capital
and hiring over time.

## Better empirical logic

The cleaner logic is dynamic rather than static.

Instead of asking whether the entrant is small at the instant of entry, ask:

- was the entrant pushed in by a worsening outside option,
- does that push margin load more on nonemployer entry than employer entry,
- conditional on entry, how quickly does the business start payroll, add employees, or
  survive?

The first question is closer to the necessity concept. The second and third questions
then describe the type of entry induced by that push.

## A more useful taxonomy

For this project, the most defensible empirical taxonomy is:

### Push-style entry

- Entry plausibly induced by worse wage-work prospects.
- Best identified through displacement, separation shocks, or other outside-option
  deterioration.

### Pull-style entry

- Entry that occurs despite strong outside options, or in response to entrepreneurial
  opportunities rather than worker distress.

### Ambiguous entry

- Entry that cannot be cleanly assigned with available data.

This is less satisfying than a universal binary label, but it is much more defensible.

## Observable implications

If the paper's mechanism is right, outside-option shocks should:

- raise nonemployer or own-account entry more than employer entry,
- reduce average initial scale conditional on entry,
- delay or weaken the transition to first payroll,
- and possibly lower early growth even if some opportunity self-employment remains in
  the data.

That pattern is much more informative than a single cross-sectional startup-size cutoff.

## Implication for the current paper

The paper should not define opportunity entrepreneurship empirically as "starts big" or
"has employees immediately." It should define the causal margin using outside-option
variation, then use business form, payroll start, capital, and survival to describe the
kind of entrepreneurship that variation induces.

## Bottom line

Inference: self-employment is not the opposite of opportunity. Opportunity
self-employment is real. The better empirical separator is not static size or capital,
but whether entry is caused by a deterioration in the outside option and how that entry
evolves after startup.
