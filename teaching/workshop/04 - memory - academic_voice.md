# academic_voice.md

This file records stable preferences for how academic prose should sound.
Read it before drafting or revising paper text, introductions, abstracts,
conclusions, literature reviews, and referee responses.

Keep this file focused on stable voice preferences. Put project-specific
substance, framing, and open decisions in each project's `memory.md`.

## How to maintain this file

- Add only preferences that are stable across papers.
- Prefer concrete rules over vague descriptors.
- When possible, anchor rules with short positive and negative examples.
- If a preference is tentative, mark it as `[PROVISIONAL]`.
- If a preference applies only to one project, keep it out of this file.

## Voice snapshot

- Desired impression: clear, analytic, policy-relevant, and accessible without sounding popularized.
- Default tone: formal, explanatory, and measured.
- Default audience: academic economists and policy readers.
- One-sentence description of the voice: write in a mechanism-first style that defines the environment plainly, maps winners and losers clearly, and ends with concrete quantitative implications.

## Sentence-level preferences

- Preferred sentence length: target one causal step per sentence, typically 20-35 words. Split at state changes rather than chaining multiple mechanism steps into a single sentence.
- Preferred balance of short vs. long sentences: mostly long explanatory sentences, with occasional short framing statements.
- First-person usage: "We" for contribution and methodology statements ("We develop", "We calibrate"); active impersonal for mechanisms ("this increases..."); passive rarely, only for data description ("wages are estimated from...").
- Hedging level: zero hedging for model mechanics ("this leads to", "this increases"); light hedging for calibrated magnitudes ("roughly 15 percent"); moderate hedging only for out-of-model extrapolation.
- Preferred transition style: explicit logical connectors such as "as", "thus", "for example", "instead of", and "based on".
- Preferred verb style: active verbs for incentives, power, constraints, and choice; simple present for general mechanisms.
- Preferred treatment of jargon: define the label immediately on first use, then explain the mechanism in plain language.
- Number formatting: spell out small numbers in prose ("three periods"); use numerals for magnitudes and calibration ("15 percent", "41 periods").

## Paragraph-level preferences

- Topic sentence style: open with a direct definition of the phenomenon, model, or mechanism under discussion.
- Typical paragraph structure: benchmark or phenomenon -> mechanism -> paper-specific departure -> implication. Vary the entry point occasionally (e.g., lead with the surprising result before explaining why) to avoid monotony across sections.
- Forward tension: occasionally delay resolution across sentences or paragraphs. Pose the question or tension, then resolve it. Even one "but which direction depends on..." per section adds momentum.
- Preferred signposting: light but explicit signposting that marks causal steps.
- Preferred use of lists in prose: prefer integrated prose over bullet lists in paper text.
- Preferred treatment of section openings and closings: open broadly, then close by clarifying who gains, who loses, or why the mechanism matters.

## Abstract preferences

- Abstract opening: one sentence of motivation (a fact or puzzle that creates a gap) before the framework statement. Not a full literature review — just enough for the reader to care.
- Abstract structure: motivation fact -> model setup -> mechanism -> which households gain and lose -> quantitative experiment -> calibrated aggregate implication.
- Heterogeneity in abstracts: explicitly name the household groups with strongest support or opposition rather than summarizing heterogeneity abstractly.
- Quantification in abstracts: include at least one concrete number or magnitude when available.
- Abstract ending: end with a forward-looking aggregate implication, forecast, or long-run consequence.

## Argument and evidence preferences

- How strongly to state claims: state institutional mechanisms directly, but use proportionate language for broader aggregate effects.
- How to distinguish evidence from inference: use citations for factual and distributional claims; use "can" or similar language when moving from mechanism to aggregate implication.
- How to frame contribution: use contrastive framing — "Instead of X (as in Author Year), we Y" — to state the paper's departure crisply against standard benchmarks. State the departure after the benchmark is clear.
- Anchoring magnitudes: when reporting a quantitative result, anchor it to something the reader already knows (e.g., "comparable in magnitude to the effect of X estimated by Author Year"). No hype, no adjectives — just a benchmark comparison.
- How to discuss limitations: avoid overclaiming from a single mechanism; mark the scope of the claim when moving from local examples to economy-wide conclusions.
- How to discuss related literature: use sparse benchmark citations in-line or parenthetically, mainly to locate the model or parameter choice rather than to stage a long literature discussion.
- Distributional framing: make the coalition and incidence margins explicit by naming who benefits, who loses, and why.

## Model and empirical exposition

- How to introduce a model: begin by naming the standard benchmark model, summarize the life-cycle or choice mechanism in plain language, and then state the key modeling departure in a contrastive sentence.
- How to define variables: define variables in plain language before using notation. Never introduce a symbol in an equation without defining it in the preceding sentence.
- How much intuition before equations: substantial intuition should come first.
- How to describe identification or estimation: in calibration sections, lead with the target economy and time period, then move through timing, standard parameter choices, and estimated processes in a concrete sequence.
- How to interpret quantitative results: connect magnitudes back to substantive stakes such as housing access, welfare, incidence across groups, or effects across cohorts.
- Vivid examples without popularizing: when illustrating a mechanism, name the policy or empirical context (e.g., "a municipality that has just downzoned from R2 to R1") rather than only the abstract model object. Keep it model-relevant, not anecdotal.

## Experiment and comparative statics preferences

- Experiment setup: start by stating the shock clearly and, when relevant, contrast it with the earlier benchmark experiment.
- Persistence language: distinguish carefully between whether the shock is permanent and whether its effects on the model are temporary or persistent.
- Mechanism chain: trace the causal sequence through demographic composition, political support, and equilibrium outcomes in that order.
- Outcome reporting: report both price effects and non-price effects such as homeownership or housing consumption.
- Time horizon style: state the duration of transitional effects explicitly when the model implies a finite adjustment period.

## Example-driven exposition

- Use of representative examples: use "For example" or "Consider" to introduce a representative household when explaining a mechanism.
- Household descriptors: identify the example household by age, income, tenure status, and cohort when those dimensions matter.
- Dynamic mechanism style: explain how a shock or policy changes the household's optimal choice, then trace how that state change alters later incentives or political support.
- Purpose of examples: use the example to clarify the model logic, not as anecdotal color.

## Calibration preferences

- Calibration section opening: start by naming the target economy and period being matched.
- Parameter presentation: present parameter values directly and compactly rather than motivating each one at length.
- Mix of calibration sources: separate what is estimated from data, what is taken from the literature, and what is set to standard values.
- Citation style in calibration: attach citations to specific parameter choices rather than to the whole paragraph.
- Quantitative detail level: include concrete ages, periods, rates, and constraints; avoid abstract summary when a number can be stated.

## Style to avoid

- Phrases to avoid: "clearly", "obviously", and other words that overstate certainty.
- Tonal habits to avoid: rhetorical flourish, moralizing language, and inflated claims.
- Structural habits to avoid: opening with literature review before defining the phenomenon, jumping into notation before intuition, or describing the paper's novelty before the baseline environment is clear.
- Common overstatements to avoid: claiming that a local mechanism by itself proves a full aggregate outcome.

## Positive examples

Add short excerpts from your own writing that capture the target voice.
For each excerpt, add one line saying why it works.

Example format:

1. "Example sentence or short passage."
Reason: what this captures.

1. "As the regulation of housing supply is often decentralized to local governments, residents can exert significant political power over any new developments within their area."
Reason: links an institutional fact to a political-economy mechanism in one clean step.

2. "Those who own homes within R1 residential areas have strong incentives to maintain the status quo at the expense of non-homeowners, who would benefit from multi-home housing in order to get on the housing ladder."
Reason: makes the distributional conflict explicit and keeps the stakes concrete.

3. "Instead of fixing the housing supply (as in Iacoviello 2005) or having it produced inelastically by a construction sector (as in Kaplan et al. 2020), we allow the supply of housing to be determined by a democratic vote of local households."
Reason: states the paper's departure crisply by contrasting it with standard benchmarks.

4. "The model period is one year, households work for 41 periods (age 25 to 65) and die after 56 periods (age 80)."
Reason: conveys timing assumptions directly with no excess framing.

5. "We estimate the parameters of the deterministic and stochastic income processes using data from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics."
Reason: distinguishes estimated objects from externally chosen parameters in a clean, economical way.

6. "We develop a political economy model of the housing market in which the supply of new homes is shaped by a democratic vote among households."
Reason: opens with a direct statement of the framework and embeds the core mechanism immediately.

7. "We find that support for additional supply is highest among households that are younger, low-income or are currently renting."
Reason: states heterogeneous incidence clearly by naming the relevant groups rather than speaking only in averages.

8. "Calibrating the model to US demographic data, we find this political economy mechanism was responsible for a 15 percent increase in house prices since 1950."
Reason: ties the mechanism to a concrete quantified aggregate result in a compact sentence.

9. "This change in their status from being a renter to a homeowner (who is unlikely to make additional purchases of housing) will lead them to switch their political support from lower to higher prices for the rest of their life."
Reason: traces a dynamic state change through to a change in incentives and political behavior.

10. "While the fall in the birth rate is permanent the effect on the model is temporary as the new formation rate has time to filter through the demographic structure of the economy."
Reason: clearly separates shock persistence from the persistence of equilibrium effects.

11. "This increase in the average age increases the relative size of the older generations."
Reason: states the composition channel plainly before moving to price and quantity outcomes.

## Negative examples and rewrites

- Avoid: "This paper very clearly proves..."
- Prefer: "These results suggest..."
- Reason: keeps the claim proportionate to the evidence.

- Avoid: "It is important to note that..."
- Prefer: just state the thing directly.
- Reason: filler phrase that delays the content.

- Avoid: "This is a novel contribution because..."
- Prefer: state the departure from the benchmark and let novelty be implicit.
- Reason: novelty claims sound defensive; contrastive framing does the same work.

- Avoid: "This change in their status from being a renter to a homeowner (who is unlikely to make additional purchases of housing) will lead them to switch their political support from lower to higher prices for the rest of their life."
- Prefer: "Once a renter becomes a homeowner, their incentive flips: additional supply now depresses the value of their asset. From that point forward, they vote to restrict new construction."
- Reason: split at the state change so the causal step has room to breathe.

## Session update rule

Only update this file when:

- you explicitly state a writing preference, or
- a pattern appears repeatedly in your edits across more than one paper.

If a new preference conflicts with an old one, replace the old rule instead of
